Colossus Engine Plan

Audio-Visualizer · engine plan · September 2026

Stage-grade visuals for any song, on one PC

Anyma's shows look expensive for two reasons: the content is made long before the show, and every visual event lands on musical time. This plan gives the visualizer both. It adds a bank of pre-produced assets, a director that predicts beats, phrases and drops, and a background forge that makes new material for each track. Everything renders through an HDR WebGPU pipeline with a fixed frame budget.

Diagnosis

Why it looks amateur today

The gap has five root causes: how content is made, how time is tracked, how light works, how reactions are chosen, and what the engine allows. Tuning the current shader can't close it.

Content

Everything is invented live

One full-screen shader builds the whole world from math, every frame. Anyma's team builds content in advance in Cinema 4D, Houdini and Unreal Engine 5. They use volumetric capture and render on cloud farms at up to 16K. The Sphere show took about four years and more than a hundred people. In the show itself, real-time work is playback, reaction and control.

Time

Time is just loudness

The app reacts to band energy. It has no beat grid, bars, phrases, sections or drop forecast. Its beat phase is a free-running metronome that is never locked to the music.

Light

Light is flat

It renders into 8-bit buffers, with no area lights, reflections or haze, and a bloom radius of 1.5 pixels. Stage visuals are mostly light: black space, haze, a few very bright sources and chrome that reflects them.

Reaction

Everything reacts to everything

Every band moves every parameter all the time, so nothing reads as intentional. Professional shows give each musical element one visual channel and keep most things still.

Engine

The engine has a ceiling

Ebiten is a 2D engine. Version 2.9.9 has no pixel-format option (8-bit RGBA only) and allows four input images per shader. It has no depth buffer, compute, instancing, storage buffers, multiple render targets or 3D textures. Each of these blocks part of the target look.

Reference

What Anyma actually does

Content

A studio pipeline builds it ahead of time. Cinema 4D and Houdini handle simulations, Unreal Engine 5 the scenes, and volumetric and motion capture the performers. Everything is rendered on custom pipelines and cloud farms, at 16K for the Sphere.

Sync

The show runs to timecode through disguise, so visuals, lighting and sound hit together. Nothing is guessed live.

Story

There is a character (Eva, the Genesys android), monumental scale and fourth-wall breaks. In one, a robot breaks out of the Sphere's LED surface, telling the story of an AI trying to get out of the box.

Copy their method

  • Make every expensive asset before it's needed
  • Timecode-grade sync, with hits landing on the audible beat
  • A hero character, monumental scale, cinematic cuts
  • Restraint: darkness, haze and a few hot lights

Win where a fixed show can't

  • Every song gets its own show, including songs nobody planned for
  • Content keeps growing per track, on your own machine
  • Room lights join the show through DMX, Hue and WLED
  • Box mode turns one screen into a window for one viewer

Thesis

Four rules the engine is built on

Pre-produce

Bake anything expensive

Simulations, lighting, geometry, textures and whole worlds are made in advance and stored in an asset bank. At runtime the engine only plays, relights, composites and directs them.

Schedule

Cue on musical time

A musical clock (beat, bar, phrase, section, drop forecast) triggers every cue. Cues fire early by the measured A/V latency, so they land with the sound. Replays use the stored song timeline, like a timecoded show.

Generate ahead

Never inside the frame

A background forge makes new material for each track against deadlines ("needed by drop 2 in 95 s"). It passes a quality gate and swaps in on a phrase boundary.

Spend on light

Pixels go to light

HDR buffers, area-light reflections, haze, real bloom and grading. A budget governor holds the frame rate so the motion stays smooth.

Architecture

Five subsystems, four clocks

The audio thread never waits on anything. The director ticks at 240 Hz on musical time, the renderer runs at display rate, and the forge works in the background against deadlines. The app stays in Go, and the renderer moves from Ebiten to WebGPU.

EAR · audio intelligence

Turn loudness into a musical clock

  • Capture. WASAPI loopback with QPC timestamps. It feeds zeros during silence, so the visuals come to rest. On Windows 11, per-app capture (build 20348 and later) keeps notification sounds out of the show.
  • Metadata. Native WinRT media-session events through winrt-go replace PowerShell polling. They give instant track changes, play and pause state, the playback position and the player's own cover image.
  • Features at 48 kHz. A 2048-point STFT with a 256-sample hop (5.3 ms) feeds 64 log mel bands. Per-band spectral flux with adaptive thresholds covers the kick, snare, hat and vocal ranges. It also tracks short-term loudness, centroid and flatness, plus chroma for key and mode.
  • Musical clock. An onset envelope gives the tempo (autocorrelation plus a comb filter), and a phase-locked loop locks the beat. Downbeats come from accent patterns and harmonic change. A phrase counter tracks 8, 16 and 32 bars. A section classifier labels intro, build, drop, break and outro. The drop forecast gives the chance of a drop in the next 1, 2 or 4 bars.
  • Latency. A one-time calibration (a flash and a click) measures the A/V offset. Cues then fire that much early, so a visual hit lands with the audible kick.
  • Song Memory. For each track (artist, title, duration) the EAR stores beats, downbeats, sections and the energy curve. On a replay it aligns them using the media-session position and onset cross-correlation, and gets exact foresight, the same as a timecoded show.
  • ML upgrades (optional). Candidates are causal beat trackers such as BeatNet or BEAST (under 50 ms latency), real-time stems like HS-TasNet or StemgenRT (about 3–6 ms delay), and All-In-One structure analysis after a first play. They run on CPU or NPU through Windows ML, or through ONNX Runtime called from Go without cgo.

Done when

  • Beat-phase error is under 20 ms at p90 on a 30-track 4/4 test set
  • 80% of drops are flagged at least one bar ahead on first play
  • Visuals are at rest within 1 s of pausing
  • A recorded-feature replay reproduces a show frame for frame

DIRECTOR · show brain

Schedule the show like a lighting designer would

  • Timeline. Show → track → section → phrase → bar → beat. Every cue is quantized to this grid.
  • Show grammar. Worlds, shots (camera rigs), cues, looks and rules live in data files that hot-reload, the way shaders do today.
  • Reaction budget. Each musical element drives one visual channel: kick to key light, snare to strobe, hats to lasers and particles, melody or vocal to the hero's glow, bass to fog and camera. An intensity level from 0 to 5 decides how many channels are live.
  • Playbook. The intro reveals the stage from darkness. The build adds tension: the strobe tracks the snare roll (capped at three flashes per second), the camera pushes in, beams converge and color drains toward white. Then comes one half-bar of darkness. The drop opens with a hard cut to a monumental wide shot and a white flash on beat 1, the hero awakens, lasers come on and the shot changes every four bars. The breakdown is slow and close. The outro pulls back into haze.
  • Camera. A physical camera (focal length and aperture) on rigs: dolly, crane, orbit and light handheld noise. Moves start on downbeats and end on phrase boundaries. The next shot's assets and pipelines load a phrase ahead.
  • Show writer (optional). On each track change, Claude returns a JSON show script chosen from the grammar's vocabulary: worlds, the shot order per section, palette, hero behavior and forge prompts. It uses Claude Opus 5.5 at low effort through the official Go SDK, with structured outputs and a cached vocabulary prompt. It picks from a vocabulary and never writes code or shaders. The rule-based director takes over if the script is late or invalid, or the app is offline.

Done when

  • Cuts land within ±1 frame of the scheduled beat
  • On first play, 80% of drops get their cut on beat 1. On a replay, 100% do.
  • No cut falls mid-phrase unless a drop forces it

RENDERER · WebGPU

Move the pixels to a real GPU pipeline

Keep Go, and replace Ebiten with WebGPU (wgpu-native, the engine behind Firefox's WebGPU) called through zero-cgo bindings. The build stays free of a C toolchain, and the app gets D3D12 or Vulkan, compute, depth, multiple render targets, instancing, indirect draws, GPU timestamps and WGSL shaders that hot-reload.

The look recipe

  • HDR all the way. RGBA16F buffers until the final 8-bit write, so highlights can burn and bloom stays physical.
  • Chrome needs something to reflect. An HDRI plus "light cards", large emissive strips like a car commercial's softboxes, rendered as analytic area lights (LTC). This is most of why a chrome android looks expensive.
  • Haze is the stage. Froxel fog lit by spots and lasers is the fastest way to say "concert".
  • Grade per world. A 3D LUT per world holds the palette to black, white and one accent at a time.
  • No banding in the dark. Blue-noise dithering before the 8-bit write. Banded near-black gradients are the most common amateur tell.
  • Scale cues. Tiny human figures at the base make anything monumental. The demo uses 110 of them.

Done when

  • 1440p holds 120 fps with p99 frame time under 8.3 ms on the target GPU
  • No visible banding in 0–5% gray ramps
  • Scene switches never hitch by more than 2 ms
  • Today's three acts run on the new renderer at visual parity

FORGE · asynchronous generation

New material for every track, made ahead of need

Nothing expensive runs on the frame's critical path. The forge computes it ahead, caches it forever and swaps it in on a phrase boundary. The musical forecast sets each job's deadline.

Deadlines

Jobs are scheduled against "needed by" times from the forecast. A job that misses its deadline falls back to the library, and the frame never waits.

Quality gate

Each asset is rendered to a thumbnail offscreen and scored by an aesthetic model plus art-direction checks (black level, contrast, palette). Rejects never reach the screen.

GPU contention

Generation runs on the NPU or CPU through Windows ML, in low-intensity sections, or in the cloud. The render thread keeps priority, and generation pauses during drops.

Cache

Everything is keyed by a hash of its inputs, so nothing is generated twice. Songs you replay get richer each time, and idle time pre-generates for recent tracks.

Frontier, optional

Real-time diffusion (StreamDiffusion reaches 55–60 fps at 512² on an RTX 4090; SANA-Streaming does 1280×704 at 24 fps on an RTX 5090) can paint "dream" breakdowns. It saturates a whole GPU, so it only runs on a second GPU or in the cloud.

Done when

A new track has its first generated asset on screen by the first drop, and no frame exceeds the budget because of generation.

VAULT · asset bank

What gets made once and reused forever

Hero pack

A chrome android bust, hands, wings, monoliths, halo rings, a cathedral and a corridor. Each comes with poses and blend shapes (awaken, breathe, turn) and baked simulations (shatter, cloth wings, fluid) as vertex-animation textures.

Sources

Poly Haven HDRIs and textures (CC0), licensed models from Fab or Sketchfab, and MetaHumans, which may be used outside Unreal since Epic's 2025 licence change. Every asset records its licence.

Formats

glTF with meshopt compression, KTX2/BasisU textures, vertex-animation textures as 16-bit images, SPZ splats. Song Memory lives in SQLite through a pure-Go driver.

Performance playbook

How great visuals stay fast

TrickWhy it pays
Render at about 67% and upscale temporallyRoughly halves pixel cost. Haze, bloom and grain hide the rest.
Reuse last frame's workHaze, ambient occlusion, raymarching and splat sorting update across several frames instead of one.
Bake what doesn't depend on audioSimulations as vertex-animation textures, lighting, SDF volumes, LUTs and noise cost nothing at runtime.
Keep particles and splats on the GPUA compute simulation plus indirect draws means the CPU never touches millions of points.
Prewarm on the musical forecastThe director knows the next shot a phrase ahead, so it compiles pipelines and streams assets then.
Compress assetsKTX2/BasisU textures and meshopt meshes cut VRAM use and upload time.
No allocations per frameUse fixed uniform structs and lock-free triple buffers between the audio, director and render threads.
Pace frames evenlyKeep one frame in flight with FIFO present. Stutter looks cheaper than a lower frame rate.
Keep ML off the render GPUWindows ML on the NPU or CPU, or the cloud. Generation pauses during drops.

Risks

What could sink it, and the plan for each

RiskMitigation
Go WebGPU bindings are betaA spike in week one, a thin renderer interface, and fallbacks: gogpu/wgpu with its Rust backend, or cgo bindings.
Art quality depends on assets and direction, not codeReference boards and look-dev come before features. Curate or commission the hero pack.
Local AI competes with rendering for the GPUThe NPU, CPU or cloud; throttling in quiet sections; deadlines with library fallbacks.
Beat tracking fails on ambient or non-4/4 musicConfidence gating. The director switches to a free-flowing mode without hard cuts.
LicencesTerms differ (TRELLIS is MIT; Hunyuan3D has regional limits; MetaHuman has its own). Record the licence of every asset in the vault.
Vendor APIs change or disappearEvery generator is optional and cached, and each has a local fallback.
Strobes and photosensitive viewersA global strobe limit (no more than 3 flashes per second), a switch to turn it off, and reduced-motion respected by default.
